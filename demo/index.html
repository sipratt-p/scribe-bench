Architecture and harness
In words
Two pipelines on the same audio
  1. Vanilla (left). Streaming recognizer (Nemotron-3.5 0.6B, 1.1 s chunks, no speaker labels) → untuned note model, plain prompt. An unoptimized baseline, not any vendor's product.
  2. Proposed (right). After-visit recognizer with a language-model decoder and speaker labels (MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize 0.9B) → an LLM step that names which speaker is the Doctor → note model with span citations → a verifier that checks every cited sentence against its lines and flags unsupported claims and third-party details. v2 adds the overnight search's picks: complaint-tuned hotwords, a precision-first instruction, DeepSeek V4 Flash as writer.
Cached vs live
  • Cached mode (pick a consultation): everything was computed once for all 57 consultations and stored; no model runs. Transcript metrics against the human transcript, note metrics against the GP's note, verifier claims, judge readouts.
  • Import mode (upload audio, "Run live"): the file goes to the GPU box; pass 1 runs on CPU, pass 2 on a GPU (~2 min for a 9-min visit); notes and the verifier run on the Mac's local models; attribution is judged by a local model. About 6 minutes end to end.
Harness
  • Data: PriMock57 (57 mock GP consultations with human transcripts, per-speaker timestamps and the GP's note); ACI-Bench for note-generation, fine-tuning and verifier studies; a 7,092-term primary-care lexicon.
  • Scorers: WER, medical-term miss, DER; term recall/precision, ROUGE-L, plan-item recall, misattributions per note, grounded/cited/linked sentences, edit effort; verifier recall on injected errors. Definitions on the measurements page.
  • Search: an overnight loop over nine pipeline knobs with a 20/37 dev/test split and a second judge as the gate; every row on the experiments page.
  • Where things run: Mac (this server, oMLX note model :8600, Gemma judge :8500) and the GPU box (vLLM endpoints, ASR runners).
This page compares two ways of turning a recorded doctor visit into the clinical note the doctor has to sign. Left is a vanilla, unoptimized pipeline: the streaming, real-time transcript written straight into a note; right adds a second, slower pass after the visit and checks the draft before the doctor sees it. Every number is measured against the human transcript and the note the real clinician wrote for that same visit.

Vanilla pipeline Unoptimized: streaming ASR (Nemotron 3.5 0.6B, 1.1 s chunks) → note. No speakers, no citations, no verifier.

A small, fast speech model listens as the visit happens and emits words within about a second. It is trained to be fast, so it has no idea who is speaking and tends to drop the rare words, which in medicine are the drug names and findings. The note is then written straight from that text.

After the visit ends there is no rush, so a different kind of speech model re-transcribes the whole recording at once. It uses a language model as its decoder, which is why it keeps rare medical words, and it labels who said what (S01, S02). The note is written from that transcript with a rule: every sentence must point at the line it came from. A separate small model then checks each sentence against the lines it points at.

Speaker labels and start times per turn. "Diarization error" measures how much of the audio got the wrong speaker; "words misattributed" is the share of words assigned to the wrong person.

Draft note, cited

The blue [[numbers]] are transcript line numbers. A clinician, or a checker, can jump from any sentence to the evidence for it. Without them the note is a black box.

Verifier

Each sentence is shown to a small judge model together with the transcript lines it cites. SUPPORTED means the evidence backs it; PARTIAL means a detail (a number, a side, a drug, a "no") isn't in the evidence; UNSUPPORTED means the claim isn't there at all. THIRD-PARTY means the note recorded someone else's health information (a spouse, a child) as if it were the patient's. Only flagged sentences are listed.

Delta on this recording

Green is the better number for the doctor. ROUGE-L is word-overlap with the note the real clinician wrote, a rough fluency and coverage score. Term precision is the share of medical terms in the draft that the clinician's note also contains: low precision means the draft mentions things the doctor would not have written. Misattributions and follow-ups come from a judge model reading the note against the transcript, the same style of evaluation described in the vendor evaluation literature.

Learnings from the full run

In plain terms: the fast transcriber drops the rare words; a slower re-transcription after the visit keeps them and knows who spoke; telling the note-writer which speaker is the doctor is what fixes who-said-what; making the note cite its evidence lets a cheap checker catch invented claims; training on more examples makes notes read better but also more likely to mix up who said what; and a bigger model on its own doesn't fix that. The overnight search loop found a few more points, and also showed how a score can be gamed: it dropped citations because the score never asked for them.

Held up when the data, the judge, or the metric changed

  1. The decoder decides medical-term loss, not latency. Running the streaming model offline changed nothing (11.8% WER either way). LLM-decoder ASR (MOSS-TD, Canary-Qwen) loses 8–10% of medical-lexicon terms; TDT/RNN-T decoders (Parakeet v3, Nemotron streaming) lose 12–15%, at the same overall WER. Same ordering on PriMock57 and on a synthetic ACI-Bench corpus.
  2. NVIDIA has the parts. Canary-Qwen matches MOSS-TD on terms; Sortformer matches it on diarization (11.1 vs 11.4% DER, ~1% misattributed words). Nano Omni prompted as a transcriber through vLLM was not usable (27% WER, invented timestamps).
  3. Anonymous speaker labels don't fix attribution in the note; naming the clinician does. Notes from the diarized transcript had the same misattribution rate as notes from the undiarized one (0.19 per note) until S01/S02 were mapped to Doctor/Patient before writing. With the role map the diarized pipeline beat the streaming reconstruction on precision, attribution and plan capture; without it the two tied.
  4. Transcript quality barely reaches the note on overlap metrics. Notes from the human transcript, MOSS-TD and Nemotron differ by 1–2 points of term recall. The note model absorbs ASR errors; what pass 2 changes is attribution, which overlap metrics never see.
  5. Citations buy grounding cheaply. Requiring a transcript line per sentence cost ~1 ROUGE-L and added 2–5 points of medical-term precision on every corpus. On the held-out PriMock set it halved unsupported sentences (11.4% → 6.8%) and left 84% of sentences checkable against the exact lines they cite. The cost is plan-item recall (81 → 76), which is the next thing being fixed.
  6. A small judge with the citation in front of it catches 99% of injected errors (fabricated findings, third-party leaks, drug swaps) with 3.5–5.5% flags on clean claims. Misses are subtle negation and number edits, which is where clinician edit deltas should train a classifier.
  7. Fine-tuning is the lever, and it has a cost. One epoch of LoRA on 2,800 open pairs moved Qwen3.8-27B +9 ROUGE-L and +12 term precision, and doubled or tripled misattributions while dropping follow-up recall up to 9 points. Overlap metrics reward fluency; clinician-defined dimensions catch what fluency hides.
  8. Scale bought safety, not fidelity. Untuned Nemotron Ultra (550B) sits a point below untuned Qwen 27B on ROUGE-L and 8 below on term precision, with the cleanest attribution of any base model. Ordering that held everywhere: tuning > base quality > size.
  9. Citations must be in the fine-tuning targets. The tune erased citation behaviour (99.6% → 10% of sentences cited) because the training notes had none.

Found by the overnight search loop: candidates, not conclusions

  1. The loop is a benchmark-maxer by construction. 76 variants scored on 20 dev consultations, promoted only if they also won on 37 held-out ones and a second judge agreed. Several dev gains failed that gate. The gate is the product; the dev score is not.
  2. What it found: +4 to +5 composite points over both baselines. Role mapping (~+0.9), switching the note writer to DeepSeek V4 Flash (~+1.6), and a precision-first instruction. Term recall did not move; the gain is fewer invented terms and fewer wrong-speaker statements.
  3. What it got wrong: it optimized citations away. The composite had no verifiability term, so citations only showed up as a recall cost and were rejected. Re-scored with a grounding term, the cited and uncited best tie on the held-out set. A score without the trust dimension in it will remove the trust feature.
  4. RL on the local writer transferred style, not content. Four rounds of expert iteration (sample 8, keep 2, fine-tune) on 20 transcripts: dev 46 → 51, held-out 41.5 → 40.5 by one judge and 44.3 → 45.9 by the other. Citations went to 99.5% of sentences and edit effort fell a quarter; term recall fell two points. The judges disagreed on the sign, which is the point: validate the judge before optimizing against it.
  5. Hotwords are a score artifact. The loop preferred complaint-tuned hotwords by about a point on the note score, but the transcript itself got slightly worse on WER, term miss and DER. Not harmful, not a real gain.
  6. Evals should be built from the edit stream. Every signed note is a labelled diff. Classify edits into safety, completeness, fit, effort, and you get the scorecard per specialty and per partner without annotators, and the same diffs become preference data. That is the whitepaper's LLM-judge programme with the training loop attached.
