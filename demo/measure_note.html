How we measure the after-visit note, and how it got better

This is the "transcribe, then write the note" mode of the demo: the recording is transcribed after the visit, the note is written from that transcript, and a checker reads the note before a doctor would. Everything below is measured on 57 recorded mock GP consultations (PriMock57) that come with a human transcript and the note the real doctor wrote, so every number is a comparison against something a person produced. Read the definitions first if the words are new; the numbers make more sense once you know what each one can and cannot see.

How to read this

There are two layers. The transcript is what the speech recognizer heard; it is scored against what was actually said. The note is what the writing model produced from that transcript; it is scored against the note the doctor actually wrote. A perfect transcript can still produce a bad note, and a mediocre transcript can produce a good one, which is why both layers are measured separately.

Two vocabulary items recur. Recall answers "of the things that should be there, how many did we get?" Precision answers "of the things we put in, how many should be there?" A note that lists every drug the patient ever took has high recall and low precision; a note that says almost nothing has high precision and low recall. A doctor needs both, but they fail in different ways: low recall means something is missing, low precision means something is invented.

Three pipelines are compared throughout. Vanilla is the unoptimized baseline: a fast streaming transcriber's output written straight into a note by an untuned model. Best is what an overnight search found: a slower after-visit transcriber that also labels speakers, a step that names which speaker is the doctor, a "prefer precision" instruction, and a different writing model. Best, cited is the same with one extra rule: every sentence in the note must point at the transcript line it came from. The numbers quoted are from the 37 consultations held out from the search, so the search could not have tuned to them.

Transcript measurements

Word error rate (WER)

What it is

The share of words the transcriber got wrong: substituted, dropped, or invented, divided by the number of words actually spoken. Ten percent means roughly one word in ten is off.

How it is measured

Both the machine transcript and the human transcript are normalized the same way (fillers like "um" removed, British and American spellings unified, numbers written the same way) and then aligned word by word.

Why it matters, and why it is not enough

It is the standard speech-recognition score, so it is what vendors quote. But most of the errors it counts are harmless ("gonna" for "going to", a dropped "yeah"). It cannot tell the difference between losing a filler word and losing a drug name.

Our numbers, all 57 recordings

Vanilla streaming transcriber 11.8%. After-visit transcriber 10.6% (10.3% without the medical-term hints). Seven different systems sat within about a point of each other.

Medical-term miss rate

What it is

Of the medical words the patient and doctor actually said (drug names, conditions, body parts, tests), the share the transcriber lost. This is the number a doctor feels when they read a transcript.

How it is measured

A list of 7,092 primary-care medical terms was built from two open medical-dialogue datasets. The human transcripts contain 1,741 occurrences of those terms; we count how many the machine transcript failed to reproduce.

Our numbers

Vanilla 12.7%. After-visit transcriber 8.6% (8.4% plain). The NVIDIA models split the same way: the ones with a classic fast decoder lost 12 to 15%, the ones with a language-model decoder lost 8 to 10%.

What we learned

This was the finding that held up everywhere: it is the type of decoder, not the speed, that decides whether rare medical words survive. Running the fast streaming model slowly, on the whole recording, did not help it at all. This is why the design has a second, different transcriber run after the visit.

Diarization error rate (DER) and misattributed words

What it is

Diarization is working out who spoke when. DER is the share of speaking time given to the wrong person, missed, or invented. Misattributed words is the simpler version: the share of words pinned on the wrong speaker.

How it is measured

The recordings have separate doctor and patient microphones, so the true speaker for every moment is known. Machine speaker labels are compared with a small tolerance around each boundary.

Our numbers

The streaming transcriber has no speaker labels at all. The after-visit transcriber: DER 11.7%, about 1% of words on the wrong speaker. NVIDIA's separate diarization model matched it.

What we learned

Knowing that there are two speakers turned out not to help the note by itself. What helped was the next step: telling the note writer which of the two is the doctor.

Note measurements

Medical-term recall and precision

What they are

Recall: of the medical terms in the doctor's real note, the share our note also contains. Precision: of the medical terms in our note, the share the doctor's note also contains. Precision is the guard against invented findings and medications.

How they are measured

Same 7,092-term list, applied to both notes, then the overlap is counted. No judgment call involved.

Our numbers, held-out 37

Recall: vanilla 52.1, best 52.7, cited 51.9. Precision: vanilla 26.4, best 30.9, cited 32.9.

What moved it

Recall barely changed anywhere, which tells you the gain was not "capturing more". Precision rose by about a quarter: fewer terms the doctor would not have written. The "prefer precision" instruction and the citation rule are where that came from. Low absolute precision is partly the data: these GP notes are terse, about 136 words, against 250-word machine notes.

ROUGE-L

What it is

A word-overlap score with the doctor's note: how much of the wording, in order, is shared. It is the standard automatic score for summaries.

Why it is treated with suspicion here

It rewards fluent, complete-looking text and is blind to who said what. Earlier in the project a fine-tuned model gained nine ROUGE points and at the same time doubled its wrong-speaker errors. So it is reported, but it never decides anything on its own.

Our numbers

Vanilla 21.8, best 23.9, cited 22.7.

Plan-item recall (completeness)

What it is

The plan is the set of actions at the end of a visit: prescriptions, tests, referrals, when to come back, when to go to A&E. This is the share of the doctor's plan items that a judge could find in our note. It is the closest thing here to the completeness dimension in clinician-defined rubrics.

How it is measured

A judge model lists the plan items in the doctor's note, then checks each one against our note. A second version only counts items that were actually said aloud during the visit.

Our numbers

All items: vanilla 80.8, best 81.2, cited 76.0. Spoken items only: vanilla 96.5, best 92.9, cited 89.4.

What we learned

The five-point drop under citations looked like the cost of citing. Then we checked the items one by one: 41 of the 154 plan items in the doctors' notes were never said in the room (a blood test ordered silently, a drug written down but not discussed). A writer can only get those by guessing, and the uncited writer sometimes guessed right. Counting only spoken items, the cited note is 3.5 points behind, not 5, and vanilla is the best of the three at capturing what was said. That is the honest open problem: the precision rules cost some completeness.

Misattributions per note (attribution)

What it is

Statements pinned on the wrong person: the patient's mother's diabetes written as the patient's, a symptom the doctor asked about recorded as one the patient reported. One of clinician-defined quality dimensions used in deployed-scribe evaluations.

How it is measured

A judge model reads our note against the human transcript and counts four kinds of wrong attribution.

Our numbers

Vanilla 0.08 per note, first two-pass design 0.11, best and cited 0.03.

What moved it

Naming the doctor. With anonymous speaker labels the note writer made as many attribution errors as with no labels at all. One added step, asking a model which speaker is the clinician before writing, cut the errors by two thirds.

Edit effort and length ratio

What they are

Edit effort is the number of character edits needed to turn our note into the doctor's note, per 100 words of the doctor's note: a proxy for how much work the doctor would do to fix it. Length ratio is our note's length divided by the doctor's.

Where they were used

On the larger ACI-Bench dataset, and as a guard in the reinforcement-learning experiment, where shorter notes were the first thing the trained model learned. Not part of the main comparison because the PriMock notes are so short that every machine note is "too long".

Trust measurements

Grounded sentences

What it is

The share of sentences in our note that a judge finds supported by the human transcript. The unsupported remainder are sentences with no basis in what was said.

Our numbers

Vanilla 88.6%, best 87.9%, cited 93.2%. So roughly one sentence in nine is unsupported in the uncited notes, and one in fifteen in the cited one.

What we learned

The uncited best is no better grounded than vanilla. Its win was precision and attribution, not fewer unsupported sentences. Citations are what halved them.

Citation coverage and linked sentences

What they are

Coverage: the share of sentences that carry a pointer to transcript lines. Linked: the share of sentences whose own cited lines actually support them, so a reader can check the claim by looking exactly where the note points.

Our numbers

Uncited notes: none. Cited: 88.8% of sentences cited, 84.0% linked.

Why this matters for the product

Commercial scribes offer on-demand evidence linking: a doctor clicks a sentence and sees the transcript. Our version generates the pointer at writing time, so a checker can run before the doctor sees the draft, and the doctor's click lands on the exact lines.

Verifier recall on injected errors

What it is

How good the checker is. We take cited notes, deliberately corrupt a quarter of the sentences (flip a "no", change a number, swap a drug, invent a finding, add a relative's private detail), and ask a small judge model whether each sentence is supported by its cited lines.

Our numbers

The checker caught 98.7 to 99.6% of the injected errors across three test sets, while flagging 3.5 to 5.5% of clean sentences. Invented findings and privacy leaks were caught every time; the misses were subtle edits like a dropped "no".

The composite, and the second judge

What it is

The overnight search needed one number to optimize. The composite blends 30% term recall, 30% term precision, 20% ROUGE-L, 20% plan recall, minus a penalty of 40 points per misattribution. It is our formula, not a vendor's.

The lesson it taught

It had no term for "is this claim verifiable", so the search threw citations away eight times: they only showed up as a small recall cost. A score that does not value the trust feature will remove the trust feature. We added grounding to the score afterwards; with it, cited and uncited tie.

Judges

All the judged numbers come from a local model reading the notes, with a second, different model confirming anything the search accepted. The two judges disagree on the size of the win: about four points by one, two and a half by the other. Neither has been checked against a real clinician, which is the step the vendor evaluation literature treats as what makes a score trustworthy.

How the numbers improved, in order

The vanilla baseline and the first design tied

A streaming transcript into an untuned note model scored 40.8 on the composite. Adding a better, speaker-labelled transcript scored 39.6. Diarization alone changed nothing the note metrics could see.

Naming the doctor

One step that maps the anonymous speaker labels to Doctor and Patient before writing. Misattributions fell by two thirds and the composite rose about two points. The first thing the overnight search accepted.

A different writer, and a precision-first instruction

Switching the note model and adding "prioritize high-precision terminology, do not invent follow-ups or medications" took the held-out composite to 45.0. Term precision went from 26 to 31. The search tried about 130 variants in total; everything else it proposed either lost on the held-out set or was a rewording of this.

Citations, rejected then reinstated

The search rejected citations because its score never valued them. Re-scored with a grounding term, the cited version tied the uncited one on the held-out set while halving unsupported sentences and making 84% of sentences checkable. The cited version is the one the demo now shows as proposed.

The plan-recall cost, explained

Four prompt changes aimed at plan completeness looked good on the search set and failed on the held-out set. The item-level check showed a quarter of the doctors' plan items were never spoken. The metric was partly wrong; the remaining real gap is a handful of safety-netting sentences per 37 visits.

Reinforcement learning, as an exercise

Four rounds of "sample eight notes, keep the best two, train on them" on the local writer moved the search set by five points and the held-out set by nothing the two judges could agree on. Style transferred (every sentence cited, notes 20% shorter), content did not. At 20 training transcripts it is memorization, and the two judges disagreeing on the sign is the reason to validate the judge before optimizing against it.

What we cannot claim

The vanilla pipeline is not any vendor's product. It is an unoptimized baseline built from open models. Deployed systems are fine-tuned on large volumes of real encounters and have their own evidence-linking features.

The judges are models, not clinicians. Every attribution, completeness and grounding number is a local model's reading. The two judges we use disagree on the size of the gain. A clinician reading a sample is the missing step.

The headline gain is not statistically resolved. Best minus vanilla on the composite is +4.2 with a 95% bootstrap interval of −0.3 to +8.8 over the 37 held-out visits. What is resolved: term precision (+4.5, interval +2.3 to +6.7), ROUGE-L (+2.2, +1.0 to +3.2), and grounding with citations (+5.3, +3.2 to +7.8). Read the composite as "probably better", not as a measured four points.

Absolute scores are low by construction. The reference notes are terse UK GP notes; the ordering between pipelines is the finding, not the level.

Medical-term hints in the transcriber were not a real gain. The search preferred them by about a point on the note score, but the transcript itself got slightly worse. Treated as noise.
